11:Tfc5,<h2>The question that started it</h2>
<p><strong>What if production systems could help us understand why they fail?</strong></p>
<p>In a real incident, the hard part is rarely finding <em>a</em> log line. It is connecting fragments of information: errors, metrics, dependencies, recent changes, and the behavior of other services.</p>
<p>An engineer may have to jump between several tools before they can even form a useful hypothesis. Meanwhile, time matters, and a confident but incorrect fix can make the incident worse.</p>
<p>After years working with large-scale backend systems, I wanted to explore whether AI could make that investigation more systematic — <strong>without taking control away from the engineer</strong>.</p>
<h2>The idea</h2>
<p><strong>Agentic Production Intelligence</strong> is an evidence-first approach to investigating production problems.</p>
<p>The system is designed to gather signals from multiple sources, connect related observations, consider possible explanations, and turn the investigation into an understandable report.</p>
<p>The ambition is not to build an agent that blindly “fixes production.” It is to build an assistant that can help an engineer answer three questions:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>What changed?</strong></li>
<li><strong>What evidence points to the likely cause and impact?</strong></li>
<li><strong>What should we investigate or do next?</strong></li>
</ol>
<h2>How the investigation is designed</h2>
<p>The workflow separates collecting evidence from interpreting it and acting on it.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Collect signals.</strong> Bring together relevant logs, metrics, service information, and other available context.</li>
<li><strong>Connect the evidence.</strong> Look for relationships between symptoms, dependencies, and recent changes.</li>
<li><strong>Form hypotheses.</strong> Explain possible causes and distinguish observations from assumptions.</li>
<li><strong>Assess impact.</strong> Describe which services or users may be affected, based on the available evidence.</li>
<li><strong>Recommend next steps.</strong> Give engineers concrete investigation or remediation options, with reasons and uncertainty made clear.</li>
</ul>
<p>The resulting report should make it possible to challenge the system&#39;s conclusions, not just accept them.</p>
<h2>Safety is part of the architecture</h2>
<p>Production is not the place for an agent to improvise dangerous actions.</p>
<p>A central design principle is that <strong>investigation and execution are different responsibilities</strong>. Sensitive operations require explicit human approval, and any decision to act should be based on fresh evidence rather than an outdated cached result.</p>
<p>The project also explores clear tool boundaries, observable workflows, and reproducible investigations — because an AI assistant used during an incident must be understandable when it gets something wrong.</p>
<h2>Why this problem interests me</h2>
<p>At eBay, I worked on distributed services and data pipelines handling millions of products, where availability, throughput, monitoring, and safe changes mattered every day.</p>
<p>That experience shapes how I think about agentic AI. An impressive demo is not enough. The system must fit the operational realities of production software: incomplete data, changing conditions, uncertainty, and accountability.</p>
<h2>Where it stands</h2>
<p><strong>In development.</strong> I am building the investigation workflow and its engineering foundations incrementally. This page describes the direction and design principles, not a claim that the system already diagnoses or resolves live incidents autonomously.</p>
<p>As the project progresses, I plan to share its architecture, realistic incident scenarios, evaluation results, and a working demonstration.</p>
<h2>The bigger idea</h2>
<p><strong>The best production AI assistant is not the one that acts fastest. It is the one that helps engineers make better decisions with evidence they can verify.</strong></p>
