11:Tbce,<h2>The question that started it</h2>
<p><strong>Can we trust an AI answer just because it sounds convincing?</strong></p>
<p>Large language models are remarkably good at producing confident, fluent answers. But fluency is not evidence. A plausible response can omit a crucial detail, mix facts with assumptions, or cite material that does not support its claims.</p>
<p>I wanted to explore a harder question than <em>“Does the answer sound right?”</em>: <strong>How can we show what is supported, what is uncertain, and why?</strong></p>
<p>That question became <strong>TruthLens</strong>.</p>
<h2>The idea</h2>
<p>TruthLens is an evidence-first AI evaluation project. Instead of treating an LLM response as the final product, it treats the response as something to investigate.</p>
<p>The goal is to break evaluation into understandable steps: identify what an answer claims, examine the available evidence, assess whether that evidence supports the claims, and present the findings in a report a human can inspect.</p>
<p>An evaluation should not end with an unexplained score. It should help someone understand <em>what happened</em>.</p>
<h2>What I&#39;m exploring</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Claim-level assessment.</strong> Looking beyond an overall verdict to the specific statements an answer makes.</li>
<li><strong>Evidence and faithfulness.</strong> Separating a well-written answer from one genuinely supported by its sources.</li>
<li><strong>Reproducible evaluation.</strong> Using defined test cases and recorded results so changes can be compared rather than guessed at.</li>
<li><strong>Model behavior.</strong> Comparing answer and evaluation models, including where the evaluator itself makes mistakes.</li>
<li><strong>Readable reports.</strong> Making questions, assessments, evidence, and limitations visible instead of hiding everything behind a single number.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Engineering approach</h2>
<p>I am building this as a Python engineering project, with explicit evaluation steps, tests, and a separation between the evaluation logic and the user-facing report.</p>
<p>One of the most interesting lessons is that <strong>the evaluator also needs evaluation</strong>. A system that judges AI answers can be wrong about extracted claims or evidence support. Measuring those errors is part of building something useful.</p>
<h2>Where it stands</h2>
<p><strong>In development.</strong> The project has an evaluation workflow and ongoing experiments, but I am still improving the reporting experience, validation, and overall usability.</p>
<p>As the work matures, this page will include a real walkthrough, measured results, examples of successful and unsuccessful evaluations, and a demo.</p>
<h2>Why it matters to me</h2>
<p>I have spent years building production systems where reliability must be demonstrated, not assumed. TruthLens is my attempt to bring that same engineering mindset to AI.</p>
<p><strong>A convincing answer is a starting point. Evidence is what earns trust.</strong></p>
